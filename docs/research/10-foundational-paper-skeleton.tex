\begin{abstract}
This paper introduces the foundational structure of a research program on open technological skill-development ecosystems. Its purpose is to define the object of study, state the main research problem, identify the principal design objectives, and prepare the conceptual-formal transition required for later axioms, definitions, propositions, and degradation analyses.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:}
skill-development ecosystems, socio-technical systems, mechanism design, applied mathematics, sustainable competition

\section{Introduction}

\paragraph{Purpose of this section.}
Introduce the general problem, explain why some ecosystems should be studied as environments for capability formation rather than merely as event or transaction systems, and motivate the need for a conceptual-formal treatment.

Some competitive systems are valuable not only because they coordinate events, but because they shape the long-term development of participants. In such cases, the central object of study is not the event itself, but the ecosystem that makes repeated participation, learning, and progression possible.

This paper is motivated by the idea that technological competition can be designed as a developmental environment. If that is true, then the relevant question is not only how to operate the system, but how to preserve the structural conditions under which capability growth, accessibility, and economic continuity can coexist.

A central intuition behind this work is that development does not emerge automatically from repeated competition. Repetition may also produce concentration, discouragement, opportunism, or exclusion. For that reason, the developmental character of the ecosystem must be treated as a design problem rather than as a byproduct of participation.

The paper therefore starts from a shift in emphasis: the relevant unit of analysis is neither the isolated participant nor the isolated event, but the evolving structure that links entry, repetition, incentives, technological experimentation, and future opportunity.

\section{Research Problem}

\paragraph{Purpose of this section.}
State the central research problem in natural language and identify the main tensions that the ecosystem must resolve.

The research problem is to understand how an open technological ecosystem can remain developmental without becoming exclusionary, opportunistic, or economically fragile. The difficulty lies in the fact that openness, competition, innovation, and sustainability do not automatically reinforce one another.

The system must therefore be designed so that its incentives and constraints preserve the conditions for skill development over time. This requires identifying which structural properties are essential and which forms of degradation arise when they are weakened.

At least four tensions appear immediately. First, lowering barriers to entry may increase heterogeneity and operational complexity. Second, rewarding strong performance may unintentionally magnify prior economic asymmetries. Third, allowing economic incentives may energize participation while also making strategic abuse more attractive. Fourth, technological freedom may stimulate innovation while simultaneously raising the cost of staying competitive.

These tensions suggest that the problem is not merely one of coordination. It is one of balance under repeated interaction. The ecosystem must be open enough to remain developmental, strict enough to remain credible, and economically structured enough to remain alive.

\section{Intuitive View of the Ecosystem}

\paragraph{Purpose of this section.}
Describe the ecosystem at a high level before any heavy formalization. Clarify what kind of object is being studied and what distinguishes it from a league, a platform, or a simple event-management system.

The ecosystem studied here is not best understood as a sequence of isolated competitions. It is better understood as a structured environment in which agents repeatedly interact, acquire capabilities, test technologies, and build trajectories of participation.

Its identity depends on continuity, progression, and role diversity. The competitive layer matters, but it matters as part of a broader developmental system rather than as an end in itself.

This means that the ecosystem must be able to connect present participation with future possibility. A newcomer should not merely be allowed to appear once; the system should make it meaningful to continue. A technical improvement should not merely affect one event; it should become part of a pathway of capability formation. A role such as participant, promoter, owner, or organizer should not remain isolated; it should become part of a larger structure of developmental mobility.

The technological nature of the ecosystem is also important. Skill is not restricted here to athletic execution. It includes design, adaptation, resource allocation, technical judgment, and strategic decision-making. For that reason, the ecosystem should be able to support both human performance and technological learning within the same developmental frame.

\section{Design Objectives}

\paragraph{Purpose of this section.}
List and organize the system-level objectives that the ecosystem is intended to preserve.

The ecosystem should not be optimized along a single axis. A system that maximizes performance while destroying accessibility is not satisfactory, and a system that maximizes openness while tolerating opportunism is also unsatisfactory.

The design objectives must therefore be treated as a constrained bundle. Accessibility, development, sustainability, enforceability, and innovation compatibility are not independent goals, but jointly necessary properties of the ecosystem.

Their relationship is not symmetric. Some objectives constrain others. Accessibility constrains how much advantage prior resources may legitimately generate. Enforceability constrains how much informality the system can tolerate. Sustainability constrains how much idealized openness can be supported without bounded commitments or recurrent support structures. Innovation compatibility constrains how rigidly the system may regulate technical experimentation.

As a result, the design objectives should not be interpreted as a checklist. They form an interdependent system of requirements. If one objective is pushed too aggressively while the others are ignored, the ecosystem may survive operationally while ceasing to be developmental in the intended sense.

\subsection{Accessibility}

Accessibility should not be understood as mere formal permission to enter. It should refer to a condition in which entry remains meaningful for agents with heterogeneous initial endowments. In later layers, this objective should be connected to the probability of sustained participation under bounded initial resources.

\begin{definition}[Accessibility]
The ecosystem is accessible at time \(t\) if there exists a non-trivial set of agents with bounded initial resources for whom entry and continued participation remain feasible with non-negligible probability.
\end{definition}

This definition should eventually be strengthened by distinguishing between nominal and substantive access. Nominal access means that entry is not explicitly forbidden. Substantive access means that entry leads to a meaningful probability of remaining in the ecosystem long enough to obtain developmental value.

A useful next step is to define, for a given resource threshold \(\bar{w}\), the bounded-resource population
\[
B_{\bar{w}}(t) := \{ i \in A : w_i(t) \leq \bar{w} \}.
\]
One may then interpret accessibility not merely through one-step admissibility, but through the existence of a non-trivial subset of \(B_{\bar{w}}(t)\) for whom feasible participation trajectories remain open over a finite horizon.

In that spirit, a later version of the paper may define a continuation-feasible set
\[
C_H(t) := \{ i \in A : \Pr[x_i(t+\tau) \text{ remains meaningfully attainable for } 1 \leq \tau \leq H] \geq \varepsilon \},
\]
for some horizon \(H\) and threshold \(\varepsilon > 0\).

Under such notation, a more informative reading of accessibility would be that
\[
\mu\big(B_{\bar{w}}(t) \cap C_H(t)\big)
\]
remains non-negligible for relevant \(\bar{w}\), \(H\), and \(\varepsilon\). The exact measure \(\mu\) remains open, but the conceptual point is clear: accessibility concerns attainable developmental continuation, not only initial admission.

This also suggests that accessibility should ultimately be evaluated relative to meaningful participation rather than mere entry. The ecosystem is not accessible in the intended sense if bounded-resource agents can appear briefly but cannot rationally remain, learn, or progress.

\subsection{Developmentality}

Developmentality refers to the existence of real pathways through which repeated participation increases relevant capabilities. These capabilities may include technical, strategic, organizational, or competitive dimensions. Later formalization should distinguish between isolated improvement and structurally supported progression.

\begin{definition}[Developmentality]
The ecosystem is developmental if repeated participation can increase at least one relevant capability variable for a non-trivial set of agents under ordinary system conditions.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Economic sustainability}

Economic sustainability should be interpreted as the ability of the ecosystem to continue operating without depending on structurally unstable external support. At a minimum, rewards, commitments, and participation incentives must remain compatible with bounded and recurrent economic conditions.

\begin{definition}[Economic Sustainability]
The ecosystem is economically sustainable if its recurrent operation and incentive structure can be maintained over time without requiring structurally unbounded external support.
\end{definition}

This notion should also be sharpened by distinguishing local solvency from structural sustainability. A system may satisfy obligations in the short term and still be unsustainable if it depends on recurrently fragile promises, unstable injections, or escalating commitments.

At a schematic level, one may think of each event \(e\) as generating an economic profile with bounded cost and bounded incentive exposure. If \(c_e\) denotes entry cost and \(p_e\) denotes the relevant incentive structure, then sustainability should eventually constrain the long-run relation between aggregate commitments, realized participation, and the recurrent viability of the event stream.

A first abstract expression may take the form
\[
\mathcal{S}(t) \sim H_S\big(\{c_e\}, \{p_e\}, \text{commitments}, \text{participation flow}\big),
\]
but later layers should distinguish at least three ideas:
\begin{itemize}
  \item bounded commitment exposure,
  \item recurrent event viability,
  \item non-dependence on structurally unbounded subsidy.
\end{itemize}

One may also anticipate a bounded-exposure condition of the form
\[
\sum_{e \in E_t} \Gamma_e(t) \leq K_t,
\]
where \(\Gamma_e(t)\) denotes effective economic exposure associated with event \(e\) and \(K_t\) denotes an admissible bound induced by the ecosystem's actual support capacity. The exact meaning of these quantities remains to be fixed, but the directional idea is that incentive promises must remain compatible with the ecosystem's recurrent support structure.

\subsection{Enforceability}

Enforceability concerns whether the ecosystem can preserve credible expectations under repeated interaction. In practice, this means that violations, opportunistic behavior, or unfulfilled commitments must have consequences that affect future participation, opportunity, or standing.

\begin{definition}[Enforceability]
The ecosystem is enforceable if violation of relevant commitments can produce future consequences sufficient to alter the expected value of opportunistic behavior.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Innovation compatibility}

Innovation compatibility means that technical experimentation and improvement must be possible without allowing the ecosystem to collapse into pure resource domination. This objective is especially important in technological competition, where creativity and cost escalation often coexist in unstable ways.

\section{Informal Axioms}

\paragraph{Purpose of this section.}
State the first structural principles of the ecosystem in plain language, before translating them into formal assumptions.

If the ecosystem is to preserve its developmental character, then some design choices cannot be treated as optional preferences. They must be regarded as structural conditions.

These conditions are introduced first in informal language because their role must be conceptually clear before they are formalized. Their function is to express what the ecosystem must preserve in order not to degrade into a different kind of system.

The reason to speak of axioms here is not that the system is already fully formal, but that certain commitments appear prior to implementation detail. For example, if future opportunity is not linked to present participation, the ecosystem may still organize events, but it no longer functions as a developmental structure. If violations carry no durable consequence, the ecosystem may still be open, but it no longer preserves trust under repeated interaction.

Informal axioms therefore serve as pre-formal commitments. They identify the conceptual backbone that later definitions and propositions will attempt to express with greater precision.

\subsection{Entry must remain open}

Open entry means that the system must preserve real permeability. New participants should be able to enter the ecosystem without requiring prior elite recognition, prohibitive capital, or privileged institutional position.

\begin{assumption}[Open Entry]
There exists a recurrent entry path through which new agents may join the ecosystem without requiring prior elite status.
\end{assumption}

In terms of the model, this assumption should eventually constrain the transition function for participation so that \(x_i(t+1)\) remains attainable for bounded-resource agents not yet active in the ecosystem.

\subsection{Participation must create future opportunity}

Participation must have developmental continuity. If current involvement has no effect on future opportunity, then the ecosystem may still host events, but it no longer functions as a progressive environment.

\begin{assumption}[Progressive Opportunity]
Current participation may affect future opportunity in at least one structurally meaningful way.
\end{assumption}

This assumption should eventually appear in the dynamics through non-trivial dependence of future participation, role, or capability variables on current participation state \(x_i(t)\) and action \(a_i(t)\).

The deeper point is that participation must be meaningful. A formally admissible but developmentally empty participation state does not satisfy the spirit of this assumption.

\subsection{Advantages must remain bounded}

Prior resources may generate advantages, but those advantages must remain bounded in a way that preserves the relevance of learning, strategy, and innovation. Otherwise, the developmental logic of the ecosystem is eventually replaced by capital filtering.

\begin{assumption}[Bounded Advantage]
Differences in prior resources may affect outcomes, but they do not fully determine developmental or competitive relevance.
\end{assumption}

Formally, this assumption should later restrict the influence of \(w_i(t)\) so that resource asymmetry does not dominate all relevant transitions or outcomes.

\subsection{Commitments must have consequences}

The ecosystem must preserve trust under repetition. This requires that commitments, promises, and obligations affect future opportunity if they are violated. A system with no durable consequence for breach may remain active while losing credibility.

\begin{assumption}[Durable Consequence]
Violation of relevant commitments may produce a persistent effect on future opportunity, reputation, or expected value.
\end{assumption}

Within the model, this means that commitment outcomes \(\ell_i(t)\) should be able to influence \(r_i(t+1)\), \(x_i(t+1)\), or both.

\subsection{Economic promises must remain bounded}

Economic incentives may be central to participation, but they must be structurally bounded. Unbounded promises increase systemic fragility and make the ecosystem vulnerable to cascades of distrust or non-fulfillment.

\begin{assumption}[Bounded Commitments]
The ecosystem restricts economic promises or obligations so that systemic exposure remains bounded.
\end{assumption}

Later formalization should connect this assumption to the event-level incentive structure \(p_e\), to aggregate system pressure \(\mathcal{S}(t)\), and possibly to admissible action sets.

\subsection{Multiple roles must be possible}

The system should allow agents to participate through more than one role over time. This matters because developmental ecosystems do not only produce better competitors; they may also produce organizers, promoters, builders, sponsors, and technical contributors.

\begin{assumption}[Role Multiplicity]
An agent may occupy more than one role across time or in different parts of the ecosystem.
\end{assumption}

This assumption should later be reflected in the dynamics of \(\rho_i(t)\), allowing developmental mobility across the role space \(R\).

\section{Formal Model}

\paragraph{Purpose of this section.}
Introduce the first formal objects of the paper: agents, roles, variables, state descriptions, and system-level quantities.

The formal model is introduced only after the main intuition has been established. Its purpose is not to replace the conceptual view, but to make it analyzable.

At this stage, the goal is to identify the minimum set of formal objects required to describe development, access, incentives, and degradation. Later versions of the paper will strengthen these objects and relate them more explicitly.

The initial model should remain deliberately minimal. If too many variables are introduced too early, the formal layer may become locally sophisticated but globally uninformative. The first task is instead to mark the main dimensions along which the ecosystem evolves: capability, resources, reputation, participation, and system-level viability.

This also means that not every important phenomenon needs immediate formal detail. Some objects may first appear as placeholders whose exact mathematical role will be refined in later layers. What matters in the present stage is to establish the analytical space within which stronger claims will later be made.

\subsection{Agents}

Let the ecosystem contain a population of agents indexed by \(i \in A\), where \(A\) denotes the set of all participating or potentially participating actors. The set should be interpreted broadly enough to include current participants, future entrants, and agents whose role in the system is not purely competitive.

The population need not be static. Entry, exit, suspension, and return should later be incorporated into the model so that the ecosystem can be studied as an evolving population rather than as a fixed competitive set.

\subsection{Roles}

Each agent may play one or more roles. At a minimum, roles may include participant, organizer, promoter, owner, technical contributor, or sponsor. The role structure matters because developmental opportunity may involve movement not only within performance levels but also across functional positions in the ecosystem.

For this reason, the role of agent \(i\) at time \(t\) may later be represented as a subset
\[
\rho_i(t) \subseteq R,
\]
where \(R\) is the role space of the ecosystem.

\subsection{Participant-level variables}

For a given agent \(i\), later layers of the model should include variables representing capability, resources, reputation, and participation state. These variables need not all be scalar in the final theory, but they are sufficient for the present layer as placeholders for the main dimensions of development.

At a minimum, one may anticipate variables such as:
\[
s_i(t), \quad k_i(t), \quad w_i(t), \quad r_i(t), \quad x_i(t),
\]
where these symbols may later denote skill, technological capability, economic resources, reputation, and participation status, respectively.

The important point is not yet the exact interpretation of each variable, but the fact that the ecosystem is dynamic. These variables are intended to evolve across time as functions of participation, incentives, technological learning, and institutional consequence.

Among these variables, \(x_i(t)\) should not be understood as a purely binary attendance indicator. In later layers it may need to distinguish inactive, nominally active, and meaningfully active participation states.

\subsection{Event-level variables}

Events should eventually be represented not merely as dates or operational units, but as structured opportunities with costs, incentives, and developmental value. Their formalization should capture both economic and competitive dimensions.

At a minimum, one may expect event-level quantities such as:
\[
c_e, \quad p_e, \quad q_e,
\]
where these may later denote entry cost, incentive structure, and developmental quality.

Later versions may refine \(p_e\) into multiple incentive components and \(q_e\) into a composite object capturing not only competitive intensity but also developmental relevance.

\subsection{Ecosystem-level variables}

The ecosystem itself should be described by aggregate or emergent quantities. These quantities are not reducible to the state of a single participant because they concern the structure of the environment as a whole.

Candidate ecosystem-level variables include accessibility, sustainability, developmental capacity, and opportunism pressure:
\[
\mathcal{A}(t), \quad \mathcal{S}(t), \quad \mathcal{D}(t), \quad \mathcal{O}(t).
\]

These variables should be interpreted as system descriptors rather than direct agent attributes. They summarize emergent conditions of the ecosystem and may depend on distributions, thresholds, and repeated interactions among agents.

At the present layer, it is useful to interpret them schematically as aggregate functions over participant states, event structures, and transition conditions. In particular, they should later be defined so that they respond not only to instantaneous counts but also to the quality of attainable future states.

A first abstract reading may be the following:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(\mathcal{A}(t)\) measures the extent to which meaningful entry and continuation remain feasible,
  \item \(\mathcal{D}(t)\) measures the extent to which the ecosystem generates progressive capability growth,
  \item \(\mathcal{S}(t)\) measures the extent to which the ecosystem can recurrently sustain its own operation and incentive structure,
  \item \(\mathcal{O}(t)\) measures the extent to which opportunistic strategies remain viable under current conditions.
\end{itemize}

To make this more explicit, one may later represent these quantities as
\[
\mathcal{A}(t) = G_A\big(z(t)\big), \quad
\mathcal{D}(t) = G_D\big(z(t), z(t+1)\big),
\]
\[
\mathcal{S}(t) = G_S\big(z(t), E(t)\big), \quad
\mathcal{O}(t) = G_O\big(z(t), a(t)\big),
\]
where \(z(t)\) denotes the global state and \(E(t)\) denotes ecosystem conditions or event opportunities at time \(t\).

\subsection{Aggregate interpretations}

The aggregate variables should eventually be grounded in observable or computable conditions rather than left purely symbolic. At the present layer, however, it is sufficient to indicate the type of structure each variable should capture.

Accessibility may later depend on the proportion or measure of bounded-resource agents for whom admissible participation states remain reachable:
\[
\mathcal{A}(t) \sim \mu\big(\{i \in A : x_i(t+1) \text{ is meaningfully attainable under bounded } w_i(t)\}\big).
\]

In a stronger version, \(\mathcal{A}(t)\) should depend not only on one-step attainability but on the availability of continuation paths over a finite horizon for bounded-resource agents.

It may therefore be useful to rewrite accessibility more explicitly in terms of meaningful participation:
\[
\mathcal{A}(t) \sim \mu\big(\{ i \in B_{\bar{w}}(t) : M_i(t) = 1 \}\big),
\]
with the understanding that both \(B_{\bar{w}}(t)\) and \(M_i(t)\) are themselves structurally induced by the ecosystem.

Developmental capacity may later depend on the expected capability gain induced by admissible repeated participation:
\[
\mathcal{D}(t) \sim \mathbb{E}\big[\Delta s_i(t) + \Delta k_i(t) \mid x_i(t), a_i(t), E(t)\big].
\]

Economic sustainability may later depend on the boundedness and recurrent viability of event-level incentives and obligations:
\[
\mathcal{S}(t) \sim H_S\big(c_e, p_e, \text{commitments}, \text{participation flow}\big).
\]

In later layers, this quantity should probably decrease when the system's effective economic exposure grows faster than its recurrent support base, or when continuation requires increasingly fragile external injections.

Opportunism pressure may later depend on the relative expected utility of strategic abuse under current enforcement and commitment conditions:
\[
\mathcal{O}(t) \sim H_O\big(\text{gain from violation}, \text{expected future consequence}\big).
\]

These expressions are not yet definitions. They indicate the kind of dependence structure that the theory should preserve.

\subsection{System objective}

At the system level, the objective is not to maximize a single output such as revenue, performance, or participation count. Instead, the ecosystem should preserve a vector of desirable properties under repeated interaction.

A first abstract representation may therefore take the form
\[
\max F\big(\mathcal{A}(t), \mathcal{D}(t), \mathcal{S}(t), -\mathcal{O}(t)\big),
\]
with the understanding that the actual form of \(F\) remains open at this stage.

This representation should not yet be read as a standard optimization problem with a single centralized controller. Rather, it expresses the idea that the ecosystem should be evaluated according to a vector of structural outcomes rather than according to a single scalar such as profit or participation count.

\subsection{State transitions}

At a later stage, the ecosystem should be modeled as a repeated system with state transitions. A generic form may be written as
\[
z(t+1) = \Phi\big(z(t), a(t), \theta\big),
\]
where \(z(t)\) denotes the global state, \(a(t)\) denotes the profile of actions taken at time \(t\), and \(\theta\) denotes structural parameters of the ecosystem.

This transition view is useful because it makes explicit that the ecosystem is not defined solely by current states but by the way present participation affects future conditions.

It also makes explicit that ecosystem-level descriptors should be studied dynamically. If the system is developmental, then the current state matters not only through what it contains, but through the range of future trajectories it enables or suppresses.

\subsection{Participant transitions}

At the participant level, one may eventually write relations of the form
\[
s_i(t+1) = f_s\big(s_i(t), k_i(t), x_i(t), a_i(t)\big),
\]
\[
r_i(t+1) = f_r\big(r_i(t), a_i(t), \ell_i(t)\big),
\]
\[
x_i(t+1) = f_x\big(x_i(t), w_i(t), r_i(t), \rho_i(t), E(t)\big),
\]
where \(a_i(t)\) denotes the action of agent \(i\), \(\ell_i(t)\) denotes relevant commitment outcomes, and \(E(t)\) denotes ecosystem conditions at time \(t\).

These relations remain schematic in the present layer, but they indicate the type of dynamical structure the paper is moving toward.

\subsection{Agent evaluation and incentives}

Because the ecosystem includes strategic behavior, it is not enough to specify state transitions alone. One must also represent, at least schematically, how agents evaluate available actions under current and expected future conditions.

At a preliminary level, one may think of agent \(i\) as comparing actions according to an intertemporal objective of the form
\[
U_i(t) = U_i\big(a_i(t), z(t), \mathbb{E}[z(t+1)], \rho_i(t)\big),
\]
where current action, present conditions, expected future state, and current role all contribute to evaluation.

A more explicit but still schematic expression may be written as
\[
U_i(t)
= \alpha_i \Pi_i(t)
+ \beta_i \Delta s_i(t)
+ \gamma_i \Delta k_i(t)
+ \delta_i V_i^{\mathrm{future}}(t)
- \lambda_i C_i(t)
- \mu_i P_i(t),
\]
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(\Pi_i(t)\) denotes immediate economic or competitive return,
  \item \(\Delta s_i(t)\) and \(\Delta k_i(t)\) denote capability gains,
  \item \(V_i^{\mathrm{future}}(t)\) denotes continuation value,
  \item \(C_i(t)\) denotes participation cost,
  \item \(P_i(t)\) denotes expected penalty or future loss induced by current behavior.
\end{itemize}

This utility expression should not yet be read as final. Its role is to identify the main channels through which ecosystem design may alter rational behavior.

\subsection{Continuation value}

The notion of continuation value is especially important in a developmental ecosystem. If present participation affects future opportunity, then agents need not evaluate actions only by immediate payoff. They may also evaluate them through expected access to later roles, events, capabilities, or reputational standing.

For that reason, one may later treat
\[
V_i^{\mathrm{future}}(t)
\]
as a function of expected reachable states under the transition system. In a simplified reading, this value should increase when current compliance or participation improves future attainability of desirable states, and decrease when current behavior damages reputation or continuation paths.

Using the language introduced later in this section, continuation value should ultimately depend on expected reachability of future states that remain developmentally valuable for the agent.

\subsection{Valuable future states}

The notion of meaningful participation depends on the idea of a valuable future state, so it is useful to introduce a preliminary interpretation. A future state should be considered valuable if it preserves or improves the agent's developmental position in at least one relevant dimension.

At a minimum, valuable future states may include states in which the agent:
\begin{itemize}
  \item retains access to continued participation,
  \item improves skill or technological capability,
  \item preserves or improves reputation,
  \item gains access to richer roles or broader opportunity sets,
  \item remains economically able to continue without prohibitive degradation.
\end{itemize}

This suggests that value should not be reduced to immediate payoff. In a developmental ecosystem, a future state may be valuable because it expands future reachability, not merely because it increases short-term return.

One may therefore later define a valuable-state set for agent \(i\) at time \(t\) as
\[
\mathcal{V}_i(t) \subseteq \mathcal{Z},
\]
where \(\mathcal{Z}\) denotes the relevant state space and membership in \(\mathcal{V}_i(t)\) depends on thresholds for continuation, capability, reputation, role, or economic viability.

At a schematic level, one may say that a future state \(z'\) is valuable for agent \(i\) if it satisfies a condition of the form
\[
z' \in \mathcal{V}_i(t)
\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
W_i(z') \geq \kappa_i(t),
\]
where \(W_i\) is a still-abstract valuation function and \(\kappa_i(t)\) is a minimum threshold of developmental relevance.

\subsection{Meaningful participation}

The paper is now using the expression \emph{meaningful participation} in several places, so it is useful to introduce a preliminary interpretation. Participation should be called meaningful when it gives the agent more than nominal presence; it must provide access to some combination of learning, continuation, role mobility, or non-trivial competitive relevance.

Accordingly, one may later distinguish between:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{nominal participation}, in which an agent is formally present but has weak developmental continuation,
  \item \emph{meaningful participation}, in which the agent retains non-negligible access to future valuable states.
\end{itemize}

A schematic condition for meaningful participation at time \(t\) may later take the form
\[
M_i(t) = 1
\quad \text{if and only if} \quad
\Pr\big(z(t+\tau) \in \mathcal{V}_i(t) \text{ for some relevant } \tau \mid z(t), a_i(t)\big) \geq \eta,
\]
for some threshold \(\eta > 0\).

This still does not fix the final content of \(\mathcal{V}_i(t)\), but it makes explicit that meaningful participation depends on developmental reachability rather than formal inclusion alone.

\subsection{Ecosystem transitions}

Because ecosystem-level variables summarize structural conditions, they should also be expected to evolve across time. A generic expression may be written as
\[
\mathcal{Y}(t+1) = \Psi\big(\mathcal{Y}(t), z(t), a(t), \theta\big),
\]
where
\[
\mathcal{Y}(t) := \big(\mathcal{A}(t), \mathcal{D}(t), \mathcal{S}(t), \mathcal{O}(t)\big).
\]

This notation emphasizes that the ecosystem has its own state of health or condition beyond the local trajectory of individual agents. In later layers, one should ask whether some regions of the ecosystem state space are self-reinforcing, fragile, or degrading.

\subsection{Stability as a system-level concern}

Because the ecosystem is dynamic, one eventually needs a notion of stability that is appropriate to developmental systems. Stability should not mean immobility. The ecosystem is supposed to evolve. Instead, stability should mean that the system remains within a region in which its developmental identity is preserved.

At a first intuitive level, one may say that the ecosystem is structurally stable over a horizon if \(\mathcal{A}(t)\), \(\mathcal{D}(t)\), and \(\mathcal{S}(t)\) remain above relevant lower bounds while \(\mathcal{O}(t)\) remains below a relevant upper bound.

This suggests introducing an admissible ecosystem region
\[
\Omega :=
\Big\{
\mathcal{Y} :
\mathcal{A} \geq \underline{A},\;
\mathcal{D} \geq \underline{D},\;
\mathcal{S} \geq \underline{S},\;
\mathcal{O} \leq \overline{O}
\Big\},
\]
where the thresholds \(\underline{A}, \underline{D}, \underline{S}, \overline{O}\) remain to be specified in later layers.

Under this interpretation, the central question is not whether the ecosystem reaches a static point, but whether its trajectory remains inside, returns to, or drifts away from \(\Omega\).

\subsection{Resilience and recovery}

If stability concerns whether the system remains within an admissible region, resilience should concern what happens after perturbation. A developmental ecosystem may suffer shocks, temporary asymmetries, opportunistic episodes, or economic stress without losing its identity, provided it can recover sufficiently.

This suggests a preliminary notion of resilience: the ecosystem is resilient over a class of perturbations if trajectories that are temporarily displaced from \(\Omega\) return to \(\Omega\) within a bounded horizon with sufficiently high probability or under sufficiently broad conditions.

At an abstract level, one may define a recovery horizon \(R\) and say that the ecosystem is \(R\)-recoverable under perturbation class \(\mathcal{P}\) if, whenever a perturbation in \(\mathcal{P}\) moves the system outside \(\Omega\), the induced trajectory re-enters \(\Omega\) within at most \(R\) periods under admissible response conditions.

This concept is useful because developmental ecosystems need not be shock-free to be viable. What matters is whether temporary degradation becomes persistent drift or recoverable disturbance.

\subsection{Interpretive note}

The formal model is intentionally being built in stages. The present layer introduces state variables and transition language without yet fixing their complete analytical form. This preserves interpretability while making the future mathematical direction explicit.

The same holds for utility. The purpose of introducing a preliminary utility representation is not to force all behavior into a narrow economic model, but to provide a bridge between structural rules and rational response. Without such a bridge, claims about opportunism, compliance, and developmental incentives remain underspecified.

The same caution applies to stability. The region \(\Omega\) is not yet a theorem-level object. At this stage it is a conceptual placeholder that organizes later questions about persistence, resilience, and degradation.

The same is true of resilience. Recovery should not be interpreted as a generic slogan about robustness, but as a later object of formal analysis tied to admissible perturbations, horizons, and return conditions.

\section{Structural Properties}

\paragraph{Purpose of this section.}
Identify the first properties that should be preserved by the system and prepare the ground for later propositions and theorems.

The ecosystem is meaningful only if some of its intended properties remain stable under repeated interaction. These properties are not merely descriptive; they are the reason the ecosystem is worth designing in the first place.

The main task of this section is to identify those properties and prepare the transition from informal design objectives to formal claims about what the system can or cannot preserve.

In this sense, structural properties play the role of invariants or quasi-invariants of the ecosystem. They need not be absolute in every local situation, but they must remain sufficiently preserved at the system level if the ecosystem is to retain its identity.

Examples of such properties include meaningful entry, non-trivial progression, bounded domination by prior wealth, and non-negligible consequences for non-compliance. Later versions of the paper should ask not only whether the system exhibits these properties under ideal conditions, but also how robustly they survive under stress or asymmetry.

\subsection{Properties to preserve}

At this stage, the main candidate properties to preserve are meaningful entry, progressive capability growth, bounded domination by prior resources, and credible future consequences for non-compliance. These should later be restated as formal properties or constraints.

Using the notation already introduced, one may interpret these properties as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item meaningful entry is linked to the attainability of admissible states \(x_i(t+1)\) for bounded-resource agents,
  \item progressive capability growth is linked to the behavior of transitions such as \(f_s\),
  \item bounded domination concerns the comparative influence of \(w_i(t)\) on relevant transitions and outcomes,
  \item credible consequence concerns the effect of \(\ell_i(t)\) on \(r_i(t+1)\) and \(x_i(t+1)\).
\end{itemize}

At the ecosystem level, preserving these properties should correspond to keeping \(\mathcal{A}(t)\), \(\mathcal{D}(t)\), and \(\mathcal{S}(t)\) sufficiently high while keeping \(\mathcal{O}(t)\) sufficiently low over time.

In particular, preserving accessibility should eventually mean preserving not only entry counts but the prevalence of meaningful participation among agents with bounded initial resources.

Because meaningful participation and continuation value have now been introduced, these properties may also be interpreted in terms of the reachability and persistence of valuable future state sets \(\mathcal{V}_i(t)\).

\subsection{Why those properties depend on structure}

These properties do not arise from participation alone. They depend on how the ecosystem links incentives, repetition, reputation, and bounded advantage. A later version of the paper should make explicit which structural conditions support which properties.

This dependence can already be stated schematically. If the transition map \(\Phi\) is modified so that some variables lose influence or gain overwhelming influence, then the preserved ecosystem properties may also change. In this sense, structural properties are not independent outputs but consequences of the shape of the transition system.

More precisely, the ecosystem-level functions \(G_A, G_D, G_S, G_O\) should later be read as induced by the transition architecture of the system. A change in admissible actions, event structure, reputation effects, or bounded commitments may therefore alter the evolution of \(\mathcal{Y}(t)\) even if the participant set remains unchanged.

This dependence also operates through rational response. If the structure of transitions and consequences changes, then the utility landscape of agents changes as well. Thus system properties are shaped both by transition equations and by the strategic choices that those equations make attractive or unattractive.

For the same reason, ecosystem stability is not purely mechanical. A trajectory may leave the admissible region \(\Omega\) because local transition rules change, because aggregate exposure grows, because continuation value collapses, or because strategic response shifts behavior in ways that amplify degradation.

\subsection{First candidate propositions}

A first family of propositions should likely connect the weakening of one structural condition with the weakening of one ecosystem property. For example, one may later ask whether the absence of durable reputational effects makes opportunism rational, or whether unbounded resource advantages destroy meaningful access.

\begin{proposition}[Need for Durable Consequence]
Suppose that for some agent \(i\) and some relevant violation outcome \(\ell_i(t)\), the transition functions satisfy
\[
\frac{\partial f_r}{\partial \ell_i}(t) = 0
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac{\partial f_x}{\partial \ell_i}(t) = 0
\]
in the sense that commitment violations do not alter future reputation or participation opportunity. Then there exist parameter regimes in which opportunistic behavior is rational.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
If a violation generates immediate gain and the violation has no effect on \(r_i(t+1)\) or \(x_i(t+1)\), then the expected future cost of opportunism may vanish or become too small. Under such conditions, the expected utility of opportunistic action can exceed that of compliant action for some incentive configurations.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Need for Bounded Advantage]
Suppose that for some relevant family of agents the transitions governing opportunity and capability become dominated by prior resources, in the sense that \(w_i(t)\) determines the attainable range of \(x_i(t+1)\) and strongly determines \(s_i(t+1)\) or \(k_i(t+1)\). Then accessibility and developmentality cannot both be preserved in the intended sense.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
If bounded-resource agents cannot access meaningful future states because \(w_i(t)\) dominates admissible transitions, then accessibility becomes merely formal. If capability growth also becomes strongly determined by prior resources, then developmental progression is no longer structurally supported for heterogeneous entrants. Thus the two objectives cannot both remain substantive.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Need for Progressive Opportunity]
Suppose that current participation state has no effect on future opportunity, capability, or role, so that \(x_i(t)\) is absent from the relevant transition structure for advancement. Then the ecosystem cannot preserve strong developmental continuity.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
If present participation does not affect future capability, role, or opportunity, then repetition has no structurally progressive value beyond isolated event outcomes. The system may remain active, but it ceases to function as a developmental pathway in the intended sense.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Need for Positive Continuation Value]
If compliant and developmental participation fails to generate sufficient continuation value for bounded-resource agents, then accessibility and retention cannot both remain structurally strong.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Suppose bounded-resource agents can enter but the expected future value of continued compliant participation remains too small relative to cost. Then entry may remain nominally feasible, but continued participation becomes unattractive. In that regime, one may observe nominal access with weak retention, which undermines substantive accessibility.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Nominal Access Is Not Substantive Access]
Suppose bounded-resource agents can enter the ecosystem, but for a non-trivial subset of them meaningful participation fails, in the sense that \(M_i(t)=0\) with high probability after short horizons. Then nominal access may be present while substantive accessibility remains weak.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Formal entry alone does not preserve developmental opportunity. If agents can enter but quickly lose access to valuable continuation states, then their participation is only nominal. In that case the ecosystem may appear open while failing to preserve meaningful bounded-resource access.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Continuation Value Depends on Valuable Reachability]
If the reachable valuable-state set \(\mathcal{V}_i(t)\) becomes negligible for a class of agents under admissible actions, then continuation value for those agents cannot remain structurally high.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Continuation value depends on the expected attainability of future states that preserve developmental relevance. If such states become unreachable or negligible in probability, then future-oriented value collapses even if nominal participation remains possible.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[System-Level Development Depends on Local Development]
If, for a non-trivial region of the state space, participant transitions fail to generate expected capability growth under repeated admissible participation, then \(\mathcal{D}(t)\) cannot remain structurally high in that region.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
If repeated admissible participation does not yield meaningful expected increases in capability variables such as \(s_i(t)\) or \(k_i(t)\), then the aggregate developmental capacity of the ecosystem cannot remain high except as a purely nominal descriptor. Thus low or vanishing local developmental gain propagates to low system-level development.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Need for Bounded Economic Exposure]
If effective economic exposure associated with ecosystem commitments becomes structurally unbounded relative to recurrent support capacity, then \(\mathcal{S}(t)\) cannot remain structurally high.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Economic sustainability requires that the ecosystem can recurrently support its incentive structure. If obligations or effective exposure grow without a corresponding bounded support base, then continuation depends on increasingly fragile conditions. In such a regime, short-term operation may persist, but structural sustainability degrades.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Admissible Region Preservation as Stability Criterion]
If the ecosystem trajectory \(\mathcal{Y}(t)\) remains within the admissible region \(\Omega\) over a relevant horizon, then the ecosystem preserves its structural identity over that horizon.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The region \(\Omega\) is constructed precisely to encode lower bounds on accessibility, developmental capacity, and sustainability, together with an upper bound on opportunism pressure. Remaining inside \(\Omega\) therefore means that the defining system-level properties remain within admissible ranges. Hence the ecosystem retains its intended structural character over the horizon considered.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Drift Outside the Admissible Region Indicates Structural Degradation]
If the ecosystem trajectory exits \(\Omega\) for sustained intervals, then at least one defining system-level property has fallen outside its admissible range, indicating structural degradation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By construction, leaving \(\Omega\) means that at least one threshold constraint on \(\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}, \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{O}\) is violated. Since those thresholds encode admissible developmental conditions, sustained exit corresponds to loss of structural integrity in at least one defining dimension.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Recovery Preserves Structural Identity After Perturbation]
If a perturbation displaces the ecosystem outside \(\Omega\) but the induced trajectory re-enters \(\Omega\) within a bounded recovery horizon, then the perturbation need not imply persistent structural degradation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The role of \(\Omega\) is to encode admissible developmental conditions. Temporary exit followed by bounded re-entry indicates disturbance without sustained loss of structural identity. Persistent degradation requires not merely displacement, but failure to recover over relevant horizons.
\end{proof}

\section{Degradation Modes}

\paragraph{Purpose of this section.}
Describe the major ways in which the ecosystem can fail if key structural conditions are removed or weakened.

The ecosystem must be studied not only through its intended goals, but also through its characteristic failure modes. A design becomes more intelligible when one can say not only what it is trying to achieve, but what kinds of collapse or distortion it is explicitly trying to avoid.

Degradation is especially important here because many harmful outcomes are structurally plausible: exclusion, wealth capture, rational opportunism, or loss of developmental meaning. These are not accidental pathologies but candidate outcomes of weak design.

Thinking in terms of degradation also prevents a common mistake: assuming that the absence of immediate failure is equivalent to health. A system may continue to operate while slowly losing openness, trust, or developmental value. For that reason, degradation must be treated as a family of trajectories, not merely as a binary failure event.

Each degradation mode should eventually be linked to one or more weakened structural conditions. This makes the theory useful not only for description but for diagnosis: it becomes possible to explain how a system drifts away from its intended form and which constraints are being lost in the process.

\subsection{Exclusion}

Exclusion occurs when entry remains formally possible but ceases to be substantively viable. In later layers, this mode should be connected to thresholds on resources, incentives, or expected continuation value.

This suggests that exclusion should eventually be linked not only to \(x_i(t+1)\), but also to the collapse of continuation-feasible sets for bounded-resource agents over finite horizons.

Under this interpretation, exclusion is compatible with visible openness. What matters is not only whether agents may enter, but whether they can remain meaningfully present long enough to access developmental value.

In state-space terms, exclusion may therefore be interpreted as a condition in which bounded-resource agents can enter the system but have little or no practical access to valuable future regions \(\mathcal{V}_i(t)\).

\subsection{Plutocratic drift}

Plutocratic drift occurs when prior resource asymmetries progressively dominate competitive relevance and developmental opportunity. This should eventually be modeled as a structural weakening of bounded advantage.

\subsection{Opportunistic drift}

Opportunistic drift occurs when strategic abuse becomes rational under the prevailing incentive and enforcement structure. This mode is likely to be closely tied to insufficient reputational consequence or weak commitment structure.

It may also be linked to poor continuation design. If compliance carries little future value while opportunism carries immediate gain, then the drift toward abuse may arise even without explicitly malicious agents.

\subsection{Economic fragility}

Economic fragility occurs when the ecosystem depends on unstable or unbounded commitments that cannot be recurrently sustained. Later formalization should connect this mode to bounded incentives and system-level continuity.

In particular, fragility should be interpreted as a trajectory in which effective economic exposure loses proportionality with recurrent support conditions, thereby weakening \(\mathcal{S}(t)\) even if visible activity remains temporarily high.

\subsection{Loss of developmental value}

Loss of developmental value occurs when the ecosystem continues to operate but no longer produces meaningful progression, learning, or mobility. This degradation mode is especially important because it may remain hidden behind visible activity.

In utility terms, this mode is associated with collapse in the future-oriented value of participation. The system may remain active in a narrow operational sense while ceasing to function as a rational pathway of growth for many of its agents.

This degradation mode is therefore closely related to the distinction between nominal and meaningful participation. A system may preserve activity, event flow, and formal openness while steadily reducing the share of agents for whom participation remains a rational developmental pathway.

In the language introduced above, loss of developmental value can be read as progressive shrinkage, inaccessibility, or weakening of valuable future state sets across relevant classes of agents.

\subsection{Instability}

Instability occurs when the ecosystem persistently drifts outside the admissible region required to preserve its developmental identity. This need not imply immediate collapse. It may take the form of repeated excursions in which one or more structural properties fail to recover.

Under this interpretation, instability should eventually be studied not only as threshold violation but also as loss of resilience: the system may continue to move, but no longer returns reliably to developmentally acceptable conditions.

\subsection{Loss of resilience}

Loss of resilience occurs when shocks, asymmetries, or temporary degradations cease to be recoverable within acceptable horizons. This mode is important because some ecosystems fail not by immediate collapse, but by progressively losing the capacity to return to admissible developmental conditions.

In this sense, resilience is a second-order property of the ecosystem. It concerns not only where the system is, but whether the system can come back from adverse displacement without losing its developmental character.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Purpose of this section.}
Clarify the scope of the present paper, distinguish what is already being claimed from what remains future work, and explain the intended audience and interpretation of the framework.

This paper is intentionally foundational. It does not aim to provide a complete mechanism, a full equilibrium analysis, or an empirical validation of the ecosystem.

Its role is to define the object of study, establish a common language, and identify the first layer of structural commitments needed for later mathematical and empirical work.

Because of this foundational role, the paper should be readable by more than one community. A reader from applied mathematics should be able to see where the formal development is heading. A reader from socio-technical systems should be able to understand the institutional and developmental meaning of the framework. A reader from competition, sport, or technology studies should be able to recognize the practical relevance of the problem.

This multi-audience ambition is not incidental. It follows from the object itself. The ecosystem under study is simultaneously mathematical, institutional, technological, and developmental.

\subsection{What this paper does}

The paper proposes the object of study, identifies the central tensions, names the principal objectives, and introduces a first structure for later formalization. Its contribution is foundational rather than exhaustive.

\subsection{What this paper does not yet do}

The paper does not yet provide a full mechanism, a complete equilibrium treatment, or an empirical test of the proposed framework. It also does not yet fix the final mathematical form of the ecosystem's variables or objective function.

\subsection{Interpretive scope}

The framework is motivated by technological competition and open motorsport, but it is intended to support broader interpretation. The present layer keeps the domain visible while allowing the theory to remain more general than any single implementation.

\section{Research Agenda}

\paragraph{Purpose of this section.}
Position the present article as the first contribution in a broader line of research and identify the next papers that naturally follow.

The present article should be read as the opening move of a larger research program. Once the ecosystem is defined conceptually and structurally, one can study specific aspects of it with greater precision.

The most natural continuations concern reputation, bounded incentives, accessibility under resource asymmetry, dynamic evolution, and empirical observation. Each of these topics can become a paper in its own right.

The order of that agenda matters. Reputation and bounded commitments should likely be addressed early because they are closely connected to enforceability and economic credibility. Accessibility under asymmetry should also appear early because it bears directly on the anti-exclusionary identity of the ecosystem. Simulation can then serve as a bridge between conceptual-formal reasoning and domain-level plausibility.

Empirical validation should arrive after the conceptual and computational layers are sufficiently articulated. Otherwise, empirical observation may remain descriptive without testing the structural claims that motivate the framework.

\subsection{Paper on reputation and compliance}

This paper should study how future opportunity, reputational memory, and non-compliance interact under repeated participation. It is likely to become the first direct extension of the foundational framework.

\subsection{Paper on accessibility versus plutocratic drift}

This paper should investigate the conditions under which openness can coexist with meaningful competition when agents begin with unequal resources.

\subsection{Paper on bounded economic promises}

This paper should clarify how incentives, rewards, and obligations can remain economically meaningful without generating structural instability.

\subsection{Paper on multi-agent simulation}

This paper should explore the ecosystem computationally under alternative rule sets, especially where analytical derivation becomes difficult or incomplete.

\subsection{Paper on empirical validation}

This paper should test whether the conceptual-formal claims correspond to observable behavioral and developmental patterns in a real implementation.

\section{Conclusion}

\paragraph{Purpose of this section.}
Close the paper by restating the object of study, the core problem, and the role of the present article as a foundation for future formal and empirical work.

The central claim of this paper is that some competitive systems should be studied as technological skill-development ecosystems rather than as mere operational platforms. This shift changes both the object of analysis and the mathematical questions that become relevant.

The contribution of the paper is therefore foundational: it proposes the object, frames the problem, and prepares the structure within which stronger definitions, propositions, and later validations can be developed.

The broader implication is that the success of such a system cannot be reduced to event execution, participation counts, or short-term visibility. If the ecosystem is truly developmental, then its quality depends on whether it preserves the conditions under which people can enter, learn, progress, innovate, and remain meaningfully connected over time.

The next layer of work should therefore move from conceptual organization to pre-formal commitments and then to the first explicit formal definitions of the ecosystem's core properties.
